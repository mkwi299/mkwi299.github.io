Trading Likes for Peace of Mind: Why Some Teens Are Choosing "Dumbphones"

I

For most teenagers today, a smartphone is the first thing they reach for in the morning and the last thing they look at before falling asleep. Yet a growing minority of secondary school students are deliberately swimming against the tide. Instead of upgrading to the latest flagship device, they are switching to basic "dumbphones" — simple handsets capable only of making calls, sending text messages, and perhaps setting an alarm. While critics once dismissed this trend as a passing novelty, recent sales figures suggest that young people are seriously rethinking their relationship with constant connectivity.

II

The main motivation behind this shift is digital exhaustion. Modern social media platforms are engineered to capture human attention for as long as possible, bombarding users with endless notifications and algorithm-driven videos. Seventeen-year-old Maya Patel, who swapped her smartphone for a basic flip phone six months ago, admits that her screen time had reached nearly seven hours a day. "I felt as though my concentration was shrinking," she explains. "Even when I was hanging out with friends in person, half of us were scrolling silently instead of actually talking."

III

Beyond improving their focus, many young users report unexpected emotional benefits. Without instant access to curated photos and constant online comparisons, feelings of anxiety and the fear of missing out tend to fade. Sleep quality also improves dramatically once glowing screens are banned from the bedroom. Interestingly, the movement has gained momentum not through lectures from worried parents or teachers, but through peer influence. In several high schools, small groups of friends have formed "offline clubs," agreeing to downgrade their phones together so that nobody feels isolated from the group.

IV

Nevertheless, abandoning a smartphone in a hyper-connected society comes with undeniable practical drawbacks. Essential everyday tasks — such as checking public transport timetables, scanning QR codes at cafés, or accessing digital school platforms — suddenly become far more complicated. Some students also worry about losing touch with classmates who rely exclusively on messaging apps to organise weekend plans. As a result, a number of teenagers have adopted a hybrid approach: they keep their old smartphones switched off in a drawer during the week and only turn them on for specific purposes.

V

It is unlikely that basic phones will ever replace smartphones entirely, nor do most young adopters expect society to turn back the clock. Instead, their experiment highlights a broader desire among Generation Z to regain control over their time and mental well-being. By proving that constant digital access is a choice rather than a necessity, these teenagers are challenging the assumption that newer technology always leads to a better quality of life.
